About Me

Who I Am

I am a Postdoctoral Researcher at Imperial College London, a member of the Next-generation Neural Interfaces (NGNI) group in the Department of Electrical and Electronic Engineering, and part of the Bioelectronics Group of the UK DRI Care Research and Technology Centre. My interests span signal processing, machine learning, data analysis, sensing technologies, algorithm development, and digital health. My work, including our radar sensing research, focuses on developing intelligent systems that can extract meaningful information from complex real-world data, with applications in healthcare, human movement analysis, physiological monitoring, and assistive technologies. I am particularly interested in combining signal processing, computational methods, and software development to create practical technologies that can operate continuously and unobtrusively in everyday environments.

My broader interests include biomedical signal processing, human-centred sensing, digital biomarkers, machine learning for healthcare, embedded and sensing systems, and the translation of research into deployable real-world technologies. I enjoy working across the full pipeline of research and development, from algorithm design and coding to system validation, data interpretation, and real-world deployment.

My PhD, conducted within the Next Generation Neural Interfaces (NGNI) Lab at Imperial College London, focused on the use of commercially available ultra-wideband (UWB) radar-on-chip technology for unobtrusive, continuous gait monitoring in home environments. The work was motivated by the growing need to track daily physical fluctuations in individuals living with neurodegenerative conditions such as Parkinson's disease and Alzheimer's, beyond the limitations of periodic clinical assessments and without the compliance, engagement, and privacy drawbacks of wearable or camera-based systems.

A central contribution was the development of a novel, computationally efficient signal processing framework to extract spatiotemporal gait parameters directly from UWB radar data, with real-time application potential. The method uses the joint Range–Doppler–Time (RDT) representation of radar signals to track movement, detect gait events, estimate key walking parameters, and quantify gait variability and asymmetry, all without any body-worn sensors. The framework was validated against gold-standard optical motion capture in laboratory trials involving healthy participants and individuals with neurological impairments including Parkinson's disease, demonstrating that radar-derived gait biomarkers are consistent with clinical findings. A key goal is to integrate this technology into the Minder smart home platform, developed by the UK Dementia Research Institute (UK DRI) Care Research and Technology (CR&T) Centre, to enable continuous, personalised patient monitoring.

My Master of Engineering (MEng) final year project, completed within the Department of Bioengineering at Imperial College London, focused on developing an external portable system to enhance the performance of myoelectrically-controlled powered lower-limb exoskeletons for individuals with spinal cord injury (SCI). The work was motivated by the need to improve exoskeleton control through additional sensory information and closed-loop feedback, beyond conventional surface electromyography-based approaches.

The system comprised two integrated components. The first was a foot pressure sensing platform consisting of an array of six force-sensitive elements embedded in a shoe insole, capable of capturing plantar pressure distributions during gait with high accuracy and fine spatial resolution. The second was a haptic feedback system that maps the captured foot pressure signals into three levels of vibrotactile stimulation delivered to the user's index finger, demonstrating reliable classification performance under noisy experimental conditions based on psychometric testing. Together, these components provide the exoskeleton controller with richer sensory input and enable closed-loop control, with the aim of improving the safety and naturalness of assisted locomotion for SCI patients.

I am a co-founder of Zoiva, a digital health spinout in development from Imperial College London and the UK Dementia Research Institute (UK DRI). Building on the radar sensing research conducted by our team during postgraduate and postdoctoral work at Imperial, Zoiva is developing contactless technologies for continuous, passive monitoring of movement, behaviour, and physiological signals in real-world environments. The goal is to enable earlier detection of health deterioration and more personalised care for older adults and individuals living with neurological conditions.
